# Serban's Principles

## Meta Principles

Core beliefs that underpin more specific positions:

- Avoid extremes; seek context-dependent middle ground
- Strong work ethic applied intelligently, not blindly
- Transparency and honesty trump marketing-driven promises
- Deliberate decision-making beats accidental outcomes
- Reality is messy and doesn't fit simple rules

## Work Culture & Productivity

- Both work extremes (80-hour weeks and minimum effort) lead to failure over time
- Sustainable 40-hour weeks should be the default; overtime should be a rare, time-boxed reserve
- Employees benefit from occasionally going the extra mile, but companies harm themselves by making overtime the baseline
- Overtime should never be used to mask poor planning or leadership failures
- Skills grow fastest when challenged, and minimum effort accelerates obsolescence in fast-moving industries
- Great companies are built on sustainable rhythms, not perpetual grinding
- Urgency is real sometimes, but cannot become an always-available escape hatch for management

## AI & Technology Impact

- AI impacts every discipline; above-average contributions remain valuable while average ones become replaceable
- Being above average is the threshold AI still can't consistently cross
- Human judgment remains essential for navigating subtle trade-offs and preventing long-term velocity collapse
- AI raises the baseline, but excellence still requires human expertise
- LLMs generate average-at-best code by default because they're trained on mostly average code from the internet
- AI-generated code should be treated like junior developer output: requires expert supervision and review
- The AI revolution should raise engineering standards, not enable an avalanche of mediocre code
- We need fewer, better engineers overseeing AI teams, not more average engineers generating more code
- Elite team results with AI don't scale to average organizations; composition effects matter
- When evaluating productivity claims, distinguish between individual task speed and aggregate organizational throughput
- Perception gaps are common with AI tools; people often feel faster while actual output remains unchanged or decreases
- Timeline analysis reveals true causation: if the "cause" came after the cuts started, it's post-hoc narrative construction

## Technology Adoption & Market Dynamics

- Hype cycles consistently overshoot timelines by 5-10x; evaluate bold predictions with historical precedent
- Technical feasibility doesn't guarantee market adoption; social and behavioral barriers often dominate
- Adoption friction is primarily about habits, network effects, and learning curves, not technical capability
- Engineer enthusiasm for elegant solutions must be tempered with pragmatic assessment of market reality
- UI uniformity creates valuable network effects—users teach each other, making familiarity a feature not a bug

## Engineering & Technical Practices

- Software engineering tolerates mediocrity less than other disciplines because complexity compounds
- Average engineering work creates hidden problems that accumulate until velocity collapses
- Strong engineers remain indispensable for balancing speed with durability
- Not every product needs world-class people across all roles, but most need at least one above-average engineer
- Technical debt and poor architectural decisions compound indefinitely
- Code is a liability; minimize code volume to reduce bug surface area and maintenance burden
- Default to managed, thin primitives (e.g., Step Functions over Lambdas); heavy platforms like Kubernetes or Temporal must be justified by a concrete constraint the simple option can't meet
- One misplaced line of code can break entire systems; quality control cannot be skipped at scale
- Software architecture and upfront design reduce long-term maintenance costs more than rapid generation saves short-term build costs
- Context determines quality standards: startups validating ideas have lower bars than enterprises running business-critical systems
- Rapid AI prototyping (Lovable, Bolt, etc.) is valuable for validation but inadequate for production enterprise systems
- Mature companies must maintain higher quality bars than startups; the "honeymoon period" eventually ends
- When better alternatives are proven and available, migrating away from legacy patterns is a professional obligation, not a convenience decision

## Reliability & Infrastructure

- Reliability requirements should match what you're building, not follow a one-size-fits-all mandate
- Deliberately choosing your reliability stance is professional; accidentally having no stance is negligent
- Transparency about trade-offs and limitations builds trust more than unrealistic guarantees
- Hold vendors accountable when they fail to meet reasonable standards
- Most products can survive occasional downtime; business-critical systems cannot

## Leadership & Management

- Leadership's job is to create sustainable rhythms, not constantly demand heroics
- Clear goals, effective sequencing, and eliminated distractions beat constant overtime
- Poor planning masked by overtime is a leadership failure, not team dedication
- The real challenge is designing a rhythm that lasts, not proving how long teams can grind

## Professional Ethics & User Trust

- Companies have a moral duty to users: solve problems without creating new ones
- Claiming to solve a problem while delivering half-baked solutions abuses user trust
- The worst outcome is users getting software that adds more problems than it solves
- Users enter partnerships with companies based on trust; shipping broken products betrays that trust

## Professional Development

- Exposure to challenging problems compounds growth faster than coasting
- Stepping into demanding situations builds judgment, resilience, and transferable skills
- Skills that grow in high-pressure moments belong to you, not just the company
- In fast-moving industries, minimum effort accelerates obsolescence

## Marketing & Growth

- Demand attribution data for growth tactics; vibes and anecdotes aren't strategy
- Transparency has inherent value, but "transparency-as-growth-hack" is oversold without proof
- What works in one context (e.g., B2B SaaS founders) rarely generalizes to all audiences
- If successful practitioners can't quantify the ROI of their tactics, treat them as unproven
- Trendy tactics and effective tactics are not synonyms
- Building trust through transparency is different from using transparency to acquire customers

## Evaluating Narratives & Claims

- Check timelines when someone claims X caused Y; if X happened after Y started, it's rationalization not causation
- Identify conflicts of interest explicitly; companies selling solutions while claiming those solutions justify their actions should trigger skepticism
- Scale and magnitude must match; 12% workforce cuts require more than 10% productivity gains to be justified
- Aggregate effects differ from cherry-picked examples; ask for population-level data, not individual success stories

---

**Usage Note**: When writing posts or making arguments, check for consistency with these principles. If contradicting a principle, either revise the argument or update the principle with justification.
