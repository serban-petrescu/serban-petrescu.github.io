Precision below 0.90 disqualifies a detector

0.90
precision: of what we flag, the share that is real. 0.95 is the goal. The hard gate.
0.90
recall: of what happened, the share we catch. Second priority.
≤2 min
latency, event to warning. Constant, never accumulating.

No single flag ever punishes a student on its own.

  • Honesty: we set these mid-build, not up front
  • Order: precision, then recall, then latency
  • Precision: of what we flagged, how much was true. Recall: of what happened, how much we caught
  • A cheater who escapes beats a student wrongly accused
  • Latency: near real time is enough, ≤2 minutes
  • Picture: green on real events; red with nothing above = false alarm (precision); dashed = miss (recall). Both 0.75 here, both fail
  • Precision never hits 1.00, not with human proctors either
  • So no single flag punishes: warnings, strikes, a human reviews any test invalidation
  • 0.90 is the entry ticket, before a detector counts at all

We build gold ten seconds at a time

  • Every week brought a new model and a new idea. Every demo looked good.
  • Picking needs a harness: run every idea against the same sessions, scored the same way.
  • Pre-trained or not: a quality claim needs a figure behind it.

We annotate in 10-second blocks to keep it simple. In around 13% of them, humans could not decide on the first pass.

  • Harness built in month five. Should have been week one: the mistake I'd most want you to avoid
  • You need to answer "is this iteration better?" That needs a gold dataset you trust completely
  • 10-second blocks, per detection type: present, clean, or undecided
  • Undecided is scored separately: a coin flip on a block six people couldn't agree on says nothing about the model
  • 13% undecided on the first pass; dropped as rules sharpened, never zero
  • Three full-time annotators keep it alive: new sessions, production findings
  • Which raises: how much do humans agree with each other?

Ask a model every 30 seconds whether anything happened, and it will tell you something did.

  • Core decision up front: one big intelligence in the middle, all context, skills, tools. Works in other products of ours. Here it failed; the rest of the talk is how
  • Attempt 1: Gemini Live, the tech behind talking to Gemini in the app. Stream video + audio, have a conversation
  • Structural mismatch: built for calls, it only answers when spoken to
  • Our shape is the opposite: two hours of nothing, then ten seconds that matter
  • Only way to get output: nudge it. And every nudge got an answer, whether or not anything happened
  • Never left engineering
  • Latency = the nudge cadence: ask every 30s, learn 30s late
  • Also didn't fit: we archive recordings for review, so everything streamed twice

Blind to motion, deaf to audio, and $1,000 per student per month.

  • Our diagnosis of attempt 1: the model is the problem
  • Best model then: Claude Sonnet
  • Backend batched screenshots: 1 composite JPEG/second, 5 per batch, 2 for context, plus a summary
  • Why the summary: a screenshot alone means nothing. Calculator allowed in one section, banned in the next, etc
  • So each call carried the previous verdicts: sequential by construction
  • Precision 0.70: below the bar, but no longer noise
  • Recall 0.20: deaf. Someone dictates answers, stills show nothing. A phone lifted between samples never existed
  • Latency killed it: 1 minute took 5 to process, compounding. After 30 min of test, 2 hours behind
  • Also absurdly expensive

Attempt 3: audio doubled recall and hid the precision gap

0.80
precision, on our annotated sessions. So we thought.
0.40
recall: audio and motion doubled it.
2x
latency: still compounding.

We shipped it everywhere, and our own number was wrong by a factor of eight.

  • Attempt 2 was deaf and never got motion from stills, however we massaged the prompt
  • From Live: Gemini does audio natively; the nudge was the problem, not the model. So: regular Gemini, not Live
  • LiveKit landed sessions on S3 in 10-second chunks, video + audio; we sent them exactly as LiveKit gave them.
  • Motion and speech in one step: recall doubled to 0.40
  • One prompt did everything: every rule, every exception, perception + judgment. It kept growing
  • Still sequential; latency from 5x to ~2x. Still behind, less
  • Precision 0.80 on our annotated sessions
  • In production until August 2026. Spring: dozens to several hundred students
  • A review team read every finding before a student saw it.

No human would ever say a student is drinking from their phone.

  • New cohort, production stopped matching the harness
  • Review team measured precision at 0.10: 9 in 10 wrong. Harness still said 0.80
  • One of them: Gemini 2.5 Flash, a ten-second video. Thermos on desk, picks it up, opens the cap, drinks
  • Model: "holding a phone and is drinking from it."
  • First instinct: another prompt line. This time we stopped
  • It falsified the premise: a LLM is not good at this
  • Not a missing rule. Any human who sees someone drink knows it isn't a phone
  • 8 similar sessions, reproduced in isolation on a three-line prompt: still a phone
  • Carried context: once one chunk said phone, the next inherited it, long after the thermos went down

Prompting harder made it worse

Every prompt line fitted the prompt harder to the videos we had handpicked.

  • How the prompt reached 400 lines: reviewer flags, engineer adds that video to the dataset, tunes the prompt
  • That loop is overfitting, with prompts instead of parameters
  • Right chart: we only ever looked at the top line. New cohort (homeschool, traditional classrooms) = the bottom line was the truth
  • Left: the three hardest sessions, worst footage, not average traffic
  • Go through the left chart.
  • Literature: the vision encoder sees it right, the language head overrules it. The largest lever is the input
  • Webcam alone: 67% → 40%. Same model, same prompt
  • Three biases, compounding: in the prompt, in the pixels, in a handpicked dataset. Then we blamed the model

Making the pipeline dumber is what raised precision

The vision pipeline, kept dumb

one window, one rule, yes or no
stateless, parallel, replayable
one question per call

Deterministic code

When did the test start and end?
Is a calculator allowed right now?
no model at all

One smart model, at the end

reads all the text, builds the story of the test
reads text only, never sees a frame
after the test, not during
  • Claim that didn't go down well: for this problem, AI is dumb
  • One uber-model failed. Three changes:
  • 1. Vision, kept dumb: one simple question at a time. Device in hand, yes/no; whether it's allowed isn't its problem.
  • One rule per question, per 60s window. No memory, no order, fully parallel.
  • 2. Deterministic code filters: test start/end come from the test service; calculator allowed = a rule in a table.
  • 3. One smart model, at the end, over text only: reads everything, reasons out the story of the test
  • Watching a test doesn't need deep intelligence. Judging one does.
  • Looks like classical computer vision: narrow perception, rules on top
  • Nothing sequential = latency stops accumulating

Separating the eyes from the judge cleared the gate

The model that decides never sees a pixel.

  • Two calls; the split is the whole point
  • Neutral narrator: no rule vocabulary. "Describe the scene, the people, the dialogue, the hands, any object, the screen." The word phone never appears
  • Classifier: gets only the text, carries all the rule words
  • Finding: loaded wording that costs 14 points in front of video costs almost nothing in front of text
  • Classifier answers booleans over the narration
  • Literature published the same split this year: presupposing questions are the largest cause of hallucination.
  • Side effect: narration is text, so new classifiers re-run over stored narrations with zero vision calls
  • Callback to attempt 2: this is the unlearning. The judge never sees a pixel

62 models auditioned for one job

Every new model gets a blind check first: what do you see? what do you hear?

  • Today: Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite, up from 2.5 Flash
  • Every Monday, an AI finds new models and scores them on precision, recall, cost, latency together
  • 3.1 Flash Lite still wins. 3.8 Flash ties on quality at 2.4x cost, so it loses
  • The funnel kills most before quality: too slow, input too large, quotas/scale don't fit; never returns the schema
  • Challenger: narration is our biggest waste. We pay a general model to reason; we need video in, description out
  • Ling + a small typed classifier: pre-checks held, <half the cost.
  • Favorite failure: we sent HLS from LiveKit. 2.5 read it fine. Gemini 3 silently drops the audio
  • Now: blind capability check first. "Worked yesterday, works tomorrow" is a bias too. Same mistake as picture-in-picture

Cheaper and better are the same direction

The cheapest system we ever ran is the most accurate one. That is not a coincidence.

  • Five systems, 18 months. X: vision cost per student per month, log, cheaper to the right. Y: precision.
  • Streaming: never measured either axis. List-price reconstruction: $280 at 30s cadence, $1,700 at 5s
  • Frames + Sonnet: $1,000/student, $3.4M/month at today's volume, 0.70
  • Video chunks: $120, $410k/month, 0.80 on our set, 0.10 in production.
  • Window graph: $26, $88k/month, 0.95. First time above the gate, 1/40th of the frames price
  • Challenger: $12, $41k/month, every floor held.
  • Direction: from "watch everything, decide everything" to classical CV: narrow perception, rules on top

Six rules we would follow if we started again

  1. Build gold before you build anything: annotate to align, and let the disagreements write the rules.
  2. Set the bar before the architecture: precision is a gate, recall a target, latency a constant.
  3. Keep it simple: no silver bullets, however strong the launch benchmarks looked.
  4. The question shapes the answer: keep rule words away from the eyes, and your own bias out of the dataset.
  5. Make the pipeline dumber: deterministic where code can decide, one binary question where it cannot.
  6. Measure the input before you judge the model: check what it actually receives, and what it perceives.

Every mistake was a bias we put there ourselves. That is the good news: so were all the fixes.

  • Ordered for someone starting today
  • 1. Gold first. We did it in month five
  • 2. Bar before architecture. We set ours halfway
  • 3. Keep it simple. The silver bullet is what gets you a 400-line prompt
  • 4. The question shapes the answer. Rule words away from the pixels; your bias out of the dataset
  • 5. Make it dumber. Code where code can decide; one binary question where it can't
  • 6. Measure the input first. Webcam at 10% of the frame, audio dropped; both times I blamed the model
  • Leave-with line: the title is a little unfair to the model. Every bias was ours: in a question, a frame, a dataset, a definition
